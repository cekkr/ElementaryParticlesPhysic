# From the Atom to the Standard Model: How and Why Every Elementary Particle Was Discovered

Nov 11, 2026 · (Claude for @Riccardo Cecchini)

## How to read this research

Every elementary particle we know was found because an earlier discovery left a question it could not answer. This research follows that chain in order. It starts with the first crack in the atom (1897), reaches the Higgs boson (2012), and ends with the questions still open in 2026.

**How each chapter works.** Each chapter answers three questions:

1. **The puzzle:** what experiment or theory did not fit?
2. **The discovery:** who found the new particle, with what instrument, and how they recognised it.
3. **The classification:** which family it was put in, and why.

**Where the story ends.** Today's Standard Model has 17 fundamental particles: 6 quarks, 6 leptons, 4 force-carrying bosons (photon, gluon, W, Z) and the Higgs boson. Each has an antiparticle, and a few (such as the photon) are their own antiparticle. Everything else (protons, neutrons, pions and hundreds of other particles) is built from quarks.

**Three ideas to hold before starting.**

- **Mass is measured as energy.** Because E = mc², physicists give masses in electronvolts (eV). The electron is 0.511 MeV, the proton 938 MeV, the Higgs about 125 GeV (1 GeV = 1,000 MeV). A heavier particle needs more energy to create, so each generation of bigger machines found heavier particles.
- **Nobody sees a particle directly.** Detectors record tracks: a charged particle leaves a trail of ionised atoms. A magnetic field bends the track, and the curvature gives the momentum and the sign of the charge. Track thickness, length and decay products reveal the mass and the lifetime.
- **"Elementary" is a provisional word.** The atom, the nucleus and the proton were each called elementary until something was found inside them. Today it means that no internal structure has been seen, down to sizes at least a thousand times smaller than a proton.

**Two engines drive the story.** Discoveries alternate between surprises and confirmed predictions. Nobody predicted the muon, the strange particles or the tau. The positron, neutrino, pion, Ω⁻, charm quark, W and Z, top quark and Higgs were all predicted before they were seen. A surprise forces theory to grow; a bigger theory predicts something new to look for.

## 1. Before particles: the atom becomes real (1808–1905)

By 1900 chemists used atoms every day, but nobody knew what an atom was made of, and some physicists doubted atoms existed at all. Four clues suggested the atom had an inside.

**Clue 1: chemistry.** John Dalton (1808) explained why compounds always form in fixed proportions: each element is made of identical atoms with a characteristic weight. Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) then arranged the elements by weight and found that their properties repeat periodically. A truly indivisible ball has no reason to repeat a pattern. Periodicity hinted at an internal structure.

**Clue 2: light from atoms.** Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen (1859–60) showed that each element emits light only at sharp, specific colours: a fingerprint called a spectrum. Johann Balmer (1885) found a simple numerical formula for the lines of hydrogen. Something inside the atom vibrates in a few fixed ways only.

**Clue 3: electricity and light.** James Clerk Maxwell (1865) showed that light is an electromagnetic wave, produced by moving electric charges. If atoms emit light, atoms must contain electric charges that move.

**Clue 4: strange rays in glass tubes.** In vacuum tubes with a high voltage, a glow travelled from the negative electrode (the cathode). These "cathode rays" divided physicists. Many German physicists thought they were waves in the ether; many British physicists thought they were streams of charged particles.

**The atom is proven real.** In 1905 Albert Einstein explained Brownian motion (the jittering of pollen grains in water) as the result of collisions with invisible molecules. Jean Perrin confirmed his predictions experimentally in 1908 and measured the number of atoms in a given amount of matter. After this, almost nobody doubted atoms.

**The questions left open around 1900:**

- What are cathode rays?
- Why do atoms emit only certain colours?
- Why do the chemical properties repeat?
- What is the energy source of the Sun, which seems to shine far longer than any chemical fire could last?

Each of these questions led, within thirty years, to a new particle or a new theory.

## 2. Breaking the atom: electron, nucleus, proton (1895–1919)

In twenty-five years the indivisible atom was split into a light, negative electron and a tiny, heavy, positive nucleus made of protons. Every step came from studying rays.

### The puzzle: mysterious rays

Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays in 1895 while studying cathode-ray tubes. Looking for a link between X-rays and glowing minerals, Henri Becquerel found in 1896 that uranium emits penetrating radiation on its own, with no energy input. Marie and Pierre Curie (1898) found two far more active elements, polonium and radium, and coined the word *radioactivity*.

Ernest Rutherford sorted the radiation by how far it penetrates. **Alpha** rays stop in a sheet of paper; **beta** rays pass through thin metal; **gamma** rays (first observed by Paul Villard in 1900) pass through centimetres of lead. These Greek letters are still used today, which is why so many particles have Greek names.

### The discovery of the electron (1897)

J. J. Thomson bent cathode rays with both an electric and a magnetic field. Balancing the two gave the ratio of charge to mass. The ratio was about a thousand times larger than for a hydrogen ion, and the same whatever metal the cathode was made of.

**Why this made it a particle, and an elementary one:** the rays carried a fixed charge per unit of mass, like identical bullets, and every element produced the same bullets. So the electron had to be a universal piece of all atoms. Robert Millikan's oil-drop experiment (1909–1913) measured its charge directly: the electron weighs about 1/1836 of a hydrogen atom. Beta rays turned out to be electrons too.

**The new puzzle:** atoms are neutral, so something positive must balance the electrons. Thomson imagined a "plum pudding": electrons scattered inside a diffuse positive sphere.

### The discovery of the nucleus (1909–1911)

Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden fired alpha particles at a thin gold foil. Most passed straight through, but about 1 in 8,000 bounced back. A diffuse pudding could never do that. Rutherford concluded in 1911 that all the positive charge and almost all the mass sit in a tiny **nucleus**, roughly 100,000 times smaller than the atom. The atom is mostly empty space.

Henry Moseley (1913) then measured X-rays from many elements and showed that each element is defined by the electric charge of its nucleus, the **atomic number** Z. This explained the order of the periodic table better than atomic weight did.

### The discovery of the proton (1917–1919)

Rutherford fired alpha particles into nitrogen gas and detected hydrogen nuclei coming out. So the hydrogen nucleus is a building block inside other nuclei. In 1920 he named it the **proton**, from the Greek *protos*, "first".

### What this chapter left open

- **The mass puzzle.** Helium has charge 2 but mass 4. Where does the extra mass come from? The favoured guess was protons plus electrons inside the nucleus (helium = 4 protons + 2 electrons). Chapter 4 shows why this failed.
- **The glue puzzle.** Protons repel each other electrically. What holds them together in a nucleus? This leads to chapter 7.
- **The stability puzzle.** By classical physics, an electron orbiting a nucleus should radiate light and spiral in within a fraction of a second. Why are atoms stable? This leads to quantum mechanics (chapter 4).

## 3. Light becomes a particle: the photon (1900–1923)

The photon was the first particle discovered by theory before it was accepted by experiment, and it became the model for every force carrier found later.

### The puzzle: hot objects glow wrongly

A hot object glows red, then white, then blue as it heats. Classical physics predicted that it should radiate infinite energy at short wavelengths, which is plainly false. In December 1900 Max Planck fixed the formula with one assumption: energy is emitted in packets, or *quanta*, of size E = h × f (h is a new constant, f the frequency). Planck saw this as a mathematical trick.

### The discovery

- **Einstein, 1905:** light itself is made of quanta. This explained the photoelectric effect: light knocks electrons out of a metal, but their energy depends on the light's colour (frequency), not its brightness, and red light below a threshold frees no electrons at all, however bright. A wave could not do this; a stream of packets could.
- **Millikan, 1914–1916:** tried to disprove Einstein and instead confirmed his prediction precisely.
- **Arthur Compton, 1923:** X-rays bouncing off electrons lose energy exactly as in a collision between two billiard balls, with the light carrying momentum. This convinced most physicists. The name **photon** was coined by Gilbert Lewis in 1926.

Light is therefore both a wave and a particle: this **wave-particle duality** applies to all particles, as chapter 4 shows.

### Why the photon matters for classification

The photon has no mass, no electric charge, and spin 1 (spin is explained in chapter 4). In quantum electrodynamics (QED), completed around 1948 by Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger, Sin-Itiro Tomonaga and Freeman Dyson, the electric force between two charges is described as an exchange of photons.

This is the key idea for everything that follows: **each force is carried by its own particle.** A massless carrier gives a force of infinite range, as electromagnetism is. In 1935 this idea led Hideki Yukawa to predict a carrier for the short-range nuclear force (chapter 7). Later it led to the W, Z and gluon. All force carriers are now grouped together as **gauge bosons**.

## 4. Quantum mechanics, spin and the neutron (1913–1932)

Quantum mechanics solved the puzzles of atomic stability and spectral lines, and it introduced spin. Spin produced the deepest division in all of particle physics, fermions versus bosons. It also showed that the nucleus could not contain electrons, which led to the neutron.

### Quantum mechanics answers the atomic puzzles

- **Niels Bohr, 1913:** electrons may occupy only certain orbits. When an electron jumps between orbits it emits one photon whose energy equals the difference. This reproduced Balmer's hydrogen formula exactly.
- **Louis de Broglie, 1924:** if light waves are particles, particles may be waves. Electron diffraction confirmed this in 1927.
- **Werner Heisenberg (1925) and Erwin Schrödinger (1926)** built the full theory. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (1927) says a particle squeezed into a small space must have a large, uncertain momentum.

### Spin: a new identity card for particles

In 1922 Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent silver atoms through a magnetic field and saw the beam split in exactly two. In 1925 George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit explained it: the electron has its own built-in angular momentum, called **spin**, of value ½ (in quantum units). Spin is as basic a label as mass or charge, and it can only be a whole or a half number: 0, ½, 1, 3/2, 2.

### The Pauli exclusion principle and the great division

Wolfgang Pauli (1925) stated that no two electrons can occupy the same quantum state. Electrons therefore fill atoms shell by shell, and the outer shells repeat their pattern. This finally explained Mendeleev's periodic table.

In 1926 Enrico Fermi and Paul Dirac showed that all particles with half-integer spin obey this rule. Earlier, Satyendra Bose and Einstein (1924–25) had described the opposite behaviour of whole-number spin particles. Their names became the two great classes:

| Class | Spin | Behaviour | Role in nature | Named after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fermions** | ½, 3/2, … | Refuse to share a state; they "take up space" | Matter | Enrico Fermi |
| **Bosons** | 0, 1, 2, … | Happily pile into the same state | Forces and fields (a laser is billions of photons in one state) | Satyendra Bose |

Pauli proved in 1940 that spin and behaviour are always linked this way. This is why, today, matter particles (quarks, leptons) are all fermions and force carriers are all bosons.

### The puzzle: electrons cannot live in the nucleus

The proton + electron model of the nucleus (chapter 2) broke down for two reasons:

1. **Uncertainty.** An electron squeezed into a nucleus would need an enormous energy, far more than the electrons emitted in beta decay actually carry.
2. **The "nitrogen catastrophe".** In the old model nitrogen-14 holds 14 protons + 7 electrons = 21 spin-½ particles, so its total spin should be a half number. Franco Rasetti measured it in 1929 and found a whole number.

Rutherford had already suggested in 1920 that a neutral particle of proton mass might exist inside nuclei.

### The discovery of the neutron (1932)

Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker (1930) found that beryllium hit by alpha particles emits a very penetrating neutral radiation, which they took for gamma rays. Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric Joliot (January 1932) showed it knocks protons out of paraffin wax with high energy. Gamma rays would have needed an implausible energy to do that.

James Chadwick repeated the experiment in February 1932, measuring the recoil of both hydrogen and nitrogen nuclei. Only one explanation fitted: a **neutral particle with almost the same mass as the proton**, the **neutron**.

### How the neutron was classified

- The nucleus became **protons + neutrons**, together called **nucleons**. Nitrogen-14 = 7 protons + 7 neutrons = 14 spin-½ particles, so its spin is a whole number, as measured. Both puzzles were solved at once.
- Heisenberg (1932) noticed that the proton and neutron have almost the same mass and feel the nuclear force equally. He treated them as two states of one particle, distinguished by a new label called **isospin**. This was the first *internal symmetry*, a hidden label that is not about space. It is the direct ancestor of the quark "flavours" of chapter 10.
- A free neutron is not stable: it decays in about 15 minutes into a proton and an electron. This beta decay became the next crisis (chapter 6).

In mid-1932 the world looked simple: protons, neutrons and electrons for matter, photons for light. That simplicity lasted only a few months.

## 5. Antimatter: Dirac's equation and the positron (1928–1932)

The positron, the first antiparticle, was predicted by pure mathematics and found in cosmic rays four years later. It doubled the number of particles at a stroke: every particle has an antiparticle.

### The puzzle: quantum mechanics ignored relativity

Schrödinger's equation works for slow particles, but electrons in heavy atoms move close to the speed of light. Paul Dirac (1928) wrote an equation for the electron that respects Einstein's relativity. It produced two surprises:

1. **Spin ½ came out automatically.** Spin was not an extra assumption; it follows from combining relativity with quantum mechanics.
2. **Solutions with negative energy.** Dirac at first interpreted the missing negative-energy states as protons. Others showed the "hole" must have exactly the electron's mass. In 1931 Dirac predicted a new particle: an **anti-electron**, same mass, opposite charge.

### Where to look: cosmic rays

In 1912 Victor Hess carried detectors on balloon flights and found that radiation increases with altitude: it comes from space. These **cosmic rays** hit the atmosphere with energies no laboratory could produce. Until the 1950s they were the main source of new particles.

### The discovery of the positron (1932)

Carl Anderson at Caltech photographed cosmic rays in a **cloud chamber** (a box of supersaturated vapour in which a charged particle leaves a trail of droplets) inside a strong magnet. A 6 mm lead plate across the chamber showed which way each particle travelled, because a particle loses energy crossing it and then curves more. One track bent the way a positive charge bends, but it was far too thin and too long for a proton. It was a positive electron. Anderson named it the **positron**.

In 1933 Patrick Blackett and the Italian Giuseppe Occhialini confirmed it, triggering their cloud chamber with Geiger counters. They photographed gamma rays turning into electron–positron pairs, as Dirac's theory predicted. Anderson and Hess shared the 1936 Nobel Prize.

### How the discovery changed classification

- **Every particle has an antiparticle** with the same mass and spin and opposite charges. The antiproton (1955) and antineutron (1956) followed. A few neutral particles, such as the photon, are their own antiparticle.
- **Particles can be created and destroyed.** A particle meeting its antiparticle annihilates into photons, and a photon with enough energy can create a pair. E = mc² works in both directions.
- **Fields become the true basic objects.** Because particle number is not fixed, physicists moved to **quantum field theory**: each type of particle is a ripple (quantum) of an underlying field that fills space. An electron is a quantum of the electron field, as a photon is a quantum of the electromagnetic field.

**The puzzle that remains:** if matter and antimatter behave almost identically, why is the universe made almost entirely of matter? This is still unsolved (chapter 14).

## 6. The beta-decay crisis and the neutrino (1914–1956)

The neutrino was invented in 1930 to save the law of conservation of energy, and it was detected 26 years later. Explaining how it is produced required a third force of nature: the weak force.

### The puzzle: energy seems to disappear

When a nucleus emits an alpha particle, the alpha always has the same energy. That is what happens when one object splits into two: the energy is shared in a fixed way. In beta decay, however, James Chadwick (1914) found that the electrons come out with *any* energy from zero up to a maximum. Charles Ellis and William Wooster (1927) confirmed that the missing energy was not hidden in gamma rays.

There was also a spin problem. A neutron (spin ½) cannot become a proton (½) plus an electron (½): two halves add to 0 or 1, never ½. Niels Bohr was ready to give up conservation of energy itself.

### Pauli's "desperate remedy" (1930)

In a letter to colleagues meeting in Tübingen in December 1930, Pauli proposed a third particle emitted in beta decay. It had to be electrically neutral, very light and spin ½. It would carry off the missing energy and fix the spin count, invisibly. After Chadwick claimed the name "neutron" in 1932, Enrico Fermi in Rome called Pauli's particle the **neutrino**, Italian for "little neutral one".

### Fermi's theory: a new force (1933–1934)

Fermi modelled beta decay on the way an atom emits a photon. In his theory, a neutron turns into a proton and *creates* an electron and an antineutrino on the spot. The journal *Nature* rejected the paper as too remote from physical reality; it appeared in Italian and German journals in 1934.

The interaction in Fermi's theory is extremely weak and acts only at very short distances. This was the **weak force**, the third known force after gravity and electromagnetism. It is the only force that changes one kind of particle into another. It also answers the old question about the Sun: hydrogen fusion in the Sun's core requires the weak force to turn protons into neutrons (Hans Bethe, 1938).

The theory also predicted the problem: a neutrino could pass through light-years of lead without stopping. Many thought it could never be detected.

### The detection (1956)

Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines needed an enormous flux of neutrinos, and found it beside the Savannah River nuclear reactor in the US. They looked for the reverse of beta decay: antineutrino + proton → neutron + positron. Their tanks of water and cadmium recorded a double signal:

1. The positron annihilates at once, giving two gamma rays.
2. A few microseconds later, the neutron is captured by cadmium, giving a second flash.

This delayed double signal is a fingerprint that background noise rarely imitates. In June 1956 they sent Pauli a telegram announcing the detection. Reines received the Nobel Prize in 1995; Cowan had died in 1974.

### How the neutrino was classified

- **Spin ½, no charge, almost no mass**: a fermion that feels only the weak force (and gravity).
- It was grouped with the electron as a particle that does **not** feel the nuclear force. This group was later named **leptons** (chapter 8).
- Neutrino and antineutrino behave differently: experiments in the 1950s led to a new conservation rule, **lepton number**, which counts leptons minus antileptons.

**The problem Fermi's theory left:** it describes the weak interaction as happening at a single point. At very high energies its predictions become impossible (probabilities above 100%). This suggested that the weak force, like electromagnetism, must have a carrier particle. That carrier, the W boson, is the subject of chapters 11 and 12.

## 7. The nuclear glue: Yukawa's meson, the muon mix-up and the pion (1935–1947)

A predicted carrier of the nuclear force was searched for, apparently found, then shown to be the wrong particle by an experiment in wartime Rome. The real one, the pion, was found in 1947. The impostor, the muon, became the first sign that nature repeats itself in copies.

### The puzzle: what holds the nucleus together?

Protons in a nucleus repel each other electrically, yet nuclei are stable. Some other force must be much stronger than electromagnetism. It must also have a very short range, about 10⁻¹⁵ m, because it does not reach beyond the nucleus. This is the **strong nuclear force**.

### Yukawa's prediction (1935)

Hideki Yukawa copied the photon idea: the nuclear force is carried by a particle exchanged between nucleons. The uncertainty principle links a carrier's mass to the range of its force. A massless photon gives infinite range; a heavy carrier gives a short range. From the size of nuclei, Yukawa estimated a mass of about 200 electron masses: between the electron and the proton. Such particles were soon called **mesons**, from the Greek *mesos*, "middle".

### The wrong particle (1936–1937)

Carl Anderson and Seth Neddermeyer, and separately J. C. Street and E. C. Stevenson, found in cosmic rays a particle of about 200 electron masses, with both charges. Everyone assumed it was Yukawa's meson.

### The Rome experiment (1943–1947)

Marcello Conversi, Ettore Pancini and Oreste Piccioni worked in Rome during the war, in hiding and under occupation. They used magnetised iron to separate positive and negative cosmic-ray mesons, then stopped them in different materials. A true Yukawa particle, feeling the strong force, should be swallowed by a nucleus almost instantly once it stops. In iron the negative mesons were captured, but in light carbon they simply decayed, as if the nuclei were not there.

Fermi, Edward Teller and Victor Weisskopf calculated that the particle interacted with nuclei roughly a trillion times more weakly than Yukawa's meson should. It did not feel the strong force at all. Luis Alvarez later named this experiment as the start of modern particle physics.

### The discovery of the pion (1947)

Cecil Powell's group in Bristol (with César Lattes and Giuseppe Occhialini) used a new tool: **photographic emulsion**, thick films that record a particle track as a line of silver grains. Exposed on mountain tops (the Pic du Midi in the Pyrenees, Chacaltaya in Bolivia), the plates showed a heavier particle, about 270 electron masses, stopping and decaying into the lighter one. The heavier one was named the **pi meson, or pion (π)**; the lighter one the **mu meson, later muon (μ)**.

The pion fitted Yukawa's prediction: it feels the strong force. In 1948 pions were produced at the Berkeley cyclotron, the first particles made in a laboratory, and the neutral pion was found there in 1950. Yukawa received the Nobel Prize in 1949, Powell in 1950.

### How they were classified, and why it mattered

|  | Pion (π) | Muon (μ) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass | about 273 electron masses (140 MeV) | about 207 electron masses (106 MeV) |
| Spin | 0 (a boson) | ½ (a fermion) |
| Feels the strong force? | Yes | No |
| Charges | +, 0, − (an isospin triplet, as Heisenberg's symmetry predicted) | − (and + for the antimuon) |
| Grouped with | Strongly interacting particles: later **hadrons**, and specifically **mesons** | Electron and neutrino: the **leptons** |

The lesson changed classification for good. **Similar mass does not mean similar nature; what counts is which forces a particle feels.** The "mu meson" lost the name meson, which was kept for strongly interacting particles of whole-number spin.

The muon behaves exactly like a heavy electron, except that it decays in 2.2 microseconds. It seemed to have no purpose. Isidor Rabi's reaction became famous: "Who ordered that?" The answer, generations of particles, took another thirty years (chapters 9 and 12).

The pion picture of the nuclear force was later understood as an approximation. The pion is itself made of quarks, and the strong force is really carried by gluons (chapters 10 and 11).

## 8. The particle zoo (1947–1964)

In under twenty years the number of known "elementary" particles grew from a handful to more than a hundred. This explosion forced physicists to sort particles into families. It also made them suspect that most of these particles could not be elementary at all.

### The surprise: strange particles (1947)

In December 1947 George Rochester and Clifford Butler in Manchester photographed two cloud-chamber events shaped like a "V". An invisible neutral particle had decayed into two charged ones. The particles were about 1,000 electron masses: nobody had predicted them. Over the next years these became the **kaons (K)** and a particle heavier than the proton, the **lambda (Λ)**. Particles heavier than the nucleons were called **hyperons** (Λ, Σ, Ξ).

**The paradox:** these particles were produced often, which means through the strong force (in about 10⁻²³ s). Yet they lived about 10⁻¹⁰ s before decaying, a typical time for the weak force. Why made strongly but destroyed weakly?

**The solution: strangeness (1953).** Abraham Pais noticed they are always produced in pairs. Murray Gell-Mann and, independently, Kazuhiko Nishijima proposed a new conserved quantity, **strangeness**. The strong force conserves strangeness, so it creates strange particles in pairs (+1 and −1). A single strange particle cannot decay through the strong force without changing its strangeness. Only the weak force may change strangeness, so the decay is slow. Strangeness became a new label on every particle, like charge.

### Accelerators take over

From the early 1950s, particle accelerators replaced cosmic rays. They give a controlled beam of known energy, millions of times per second.

- **Cosmotron** (Brookhaven, 1953, 3.3 GeV): first to produce strange particles at will, confirming pair production.
- **Bevatron** (Berkeley, 1954, 6.2 GeV): built with just enough energy to create proton–antiproton pairs. In 1955 Emilio Segrè, Owen Chamberlain, Clyde Wiegand and Thomas Ypsilantis found the **antiproton**, confirming that Dirac's antimatter applies to all particles. The antineutron followed in 1956.
- **Bubble chamber** (invented by Donald Glaser, 1952): a tank of superheated liquid in which particles leave trails of bubbles. Luis Alvarez's large liquid-hydrogen chambers at Berkeley recorded millions of photographs, scanned semi-automatically.

### Resonances: particles that live too briefly to leave a track

In 1952 Fermi's group in Chicago scattered pions off protons. The collision rate peaked sharply at a total energy of about 1,232 MeV. This peak is a particle, now called the **Δ (Delta)**, which lives only about 10⁻²³ s. It travels less than a proton's width, so it leaves no track; it shows up only as a bump in a graph of energies. In the 1960s, bubble chambers revealed dozens of such **resonances** (ρ, ω, K\*, Σ\*, …). Fermi reportedly joked that if he could remember all their names he would have become a botanist.

### The first classification: by mass, then by force

Physicists first sorted particles by mass, and gave the groups Greek names:

| Group | Greek root | Original criterion | Modern definition | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Leptons** | *leptos*, light or small | Lightest particles | Spin-½ particles that do **not** feel the strong force | electron, muon, neutrinos |
| **Mesons** | *mesos*, middle | Intermediate mass | Strongly interacting particles with **whole-number spin** (baryon number 0) | π, K, ρ |
| **Baryons** | *barys*, heavy | Heaviest particles | Strongly interacting particles with **half-number spin** (baryon number 1) | proton, neutron, Λ, Σ, Ξ, Δ |
| **Hadrons** | *hadros*, thick or strong | (name from Lev Okun, 1962) | Everything that feels the strong force = mesons + baryons | all of the above except leptons |

The mass-based names soon became misleading: the tau lepton (chapter 12) is heavier than the proton, and some mesons are heavier than most baryons. The names were kept, but the definitions changed from **"how heavy"** to **"which forces it feels and what spin it has"**.

New **conservation laws** also became sorting tools. **Baryon number** (+1 for baryons, −1 for antibaryons) is conserved, which is why the proton, the lightest baryon, cannot decay. **Lepton number** does the same for leptons, and **strangeness** and **isospin** group hadrons into families.

### The hint of something deeper

Two clues suggested hadrons were not elementary:

1. **Too many of them.** A hundred "elementary" particles resembled the many chemical elements before the periodic table: a sign of hidden building blocks.
2. **They have a size.** Robert Hofstadter (Stanford, 1950s) bounced electrons off protons and measured a radius of about 0.8 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, with charge spread out inside. Leptons, by contrast, behaved as points.

The search for order among the hadrons is the subject of chapter 10. Before that, a crisis came from the weak force.

## 9. Broken mirrors: parity, two neutrinos and CP violation (1956–1964)

Between 1956 and 1964, three experiments showed that the weak force distinguishes left from right and matter from antimatter. They also showed that leptons come in separate families. These results became the rules that shaped the Standard Model.

### The puzzle: one particle or two? (the θ–τ puzzle)

Two strange particles, called θ and τ (this τ is unrelated to today's tau lepton), had identical mass and lifetime. But θ decayed into two pions and τ into three. These final states have opposite **parity**, a property that says how a system behaves when reflected in a mirror. If nature is mirror-symmetric, θ and τ had to be different particles. Yet everything else about them was identical.

### Parity violation (1956–1957)

Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang checked the evidence in 1956. They found that mirror symmetry had never been tested for the weak force, and proposed experiments to test it.

Chien-Shiung Wu and colleagues at the US National Bureau of Standards cooled cobalt-60 to a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero and lined up the nuclear spins with a magnetic field. The beta-decay electrons came out mostly in one direction relative to the spin. A mirror image of the experiment would show the opposite, so **the weak force is not mirror-symmetric**. Richard Garwin, Leon Lederman and Marcel Weinrich confirmed it within days using pion and muon decays. Lee and Yang received the Nobel Prize in 1957; Wu was not included.

**Consequences for classification:**

- θ and τ were the same particle, the charged kaon.
- The weak force acts only on **left-handed** particles (those spinning one way relative to their motion). Maurice Goldhaber (1958) measured that neutrinos are always left-handed.
- In the Standard Model, left-handed and right-handed versions of each matter particle are treated as different objects. Left-handed ones come in **pairs (doublets)** that the weak force connects, such as (electron-neutrino, electron). Right-handed ones are **singlets** that the weak force ignores.

### Two neutrinos (1962)

**The puzzle:** a muon never decays into an electron plus a photon (μ → eγ), although with only one kind of neutrino theory said it should happen. Perhaps the neutrino produced with a muon differs from the one produced with an electron.

Leon Lederman, Melvin Schwartz and Jack Steinberger built the first **accelerator neutrino beam** at Brookhaven. Protons hit a target to make pions, which decay into muons and neutrinos; about 13 m of steel stopped everything except the neutrinos. When those neutrinos interacted, they produced muons but no electrons. So **the muon neutrino (νμ) is a different particle from the electron neutrino (νe)**. Nobel Prize 1988.

**Consequence:** leptons come in **families**: (e, νe) and (μ, νμ). Each family has its own conserved number. The muon now had a partner and a place: it was the heavy copy of the electron in a second family. In 1964 James Bjorken and Sheldon Glashow suggested that quarks should have a matching second family (chapter 10).

### CP violation (1964)

After 1957 physicists hoped a combined symmetry survived: swap particles with antiparticles (C) **and** reflect in a mirror (P). This **CP** symmetry would mean matter and antimatter are exact mirror twins.

James Cronin, Val Fitch, James Christenson and René Turlay studied the long-lived neutral kaon at Brookhaven. If CP held, it could decay only into three pions. About 2 times in 1,000, it decayed into two. **CP is violated**: matter and antimatter are slightly different. Nobel Prize 1980.

**Why this matters:**

- Andrei Sakharov (1967) showed that CP violation is one of three conditions needed for the early universe to produce more matter than antimatter.
- Makoto Kobayashi and Toshihide Maskawa (1973) showed that the Standard Model can produce CP violation only if there are at least **three families** of quarks. When they wrote this, only three quarks (one and a half families) were known. Their prediction is confirmed in chapter 12.

## 10. Order from chaos: the Eightfold Way, quarks and colour (1961–1973)

The hundred hadrons turned out to be combinations of just three smaller particles, **quarks**, held together by a new kind of charge called **colour**. The proof came in five steps: a pattern, a prediction, a model, a correction and a direct look inside the proton.

### Step 1: the pattern (1961)

Murray Gell-Mann and Yuval Ne'eman, independently, plotted hadrons of the same spin on a chart of strangeness against electric charge. The particles fell into regular geometric shapes: hexagons of 8 (**octets**) and triangles of 10 (**decuplets**). The mathematics behind the shapes is a symmetry group called SU(3). Gell-Mann named the scheme the **Eightfold Way**, after the Buddhist Noble Eightfold Path.

- **Spin-½ baryon octet:** p, n, Λ, Σ⁺, Σ⁰, Σ⁻, Ξ⁰, Ξ⁻
- **Spin-0 meson octet:** π⁺, π⁰, π⁻, K⁺, K⁰, anti-K⁰, K⁻, η

### Step 2: the prediction of the Ω⁻ (1962–1964)

The spin-3/2 baryons formed a triangle of 10 places, but only 9 particles were known. At a CERN conference in 1962 Gell-Mann predicted the missing one: charge −1, strangeness −3, a mass about 150 MeV above the row before it, and a long life, because it could decay only through the weak force. In February 1964 a Brookhaven bubble-chamber team led by Nicholas Samios found it in a single photograph out of tens of thousands. It was the particle-physics equivalent of Mendeleev predicting unknown elements from the gaps in his table.

&#91;embedded content: The baryon decuplet of the Eightfold Way · 10 particles, 4 strangeness levels\]

The pattern works because each step down the triangle swaps one light quark for a heavier strange quark (step 3 explains the quark names).

### Step 3: quarks (1964)

Why do these shapes appear? Gell-Mann and George Zweig (at CERN) proposed, independently, that all hadrons are built from three smaller particles with **fractional electric charge**. Gell-Mann called them **quarks**, a word taken from a line in James Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*. Zweig called them "aces".

| Quark | Symbol | Electric charge | Strangeness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| up | u | +2/3 | 0 |
| down | d | −1/3 | 0 |
| strange | s | −1/3 | −1 |

Two rules then explain all the hadrons known in 1964:

- **Baryons = three quarks:** proton = uud (2/3 + 2/3 − 1/3 = +1), neutron = udd (0), Λ = uds, Ω⁻ = sss.
- **Mesons = one quark + one antiquark:** π⁺ = u + anti-d, K⁺ = u + anti-s.

At once, old labels found a meaning. Strangeness counts strange quarks. Isospin reflects the near-equal masses of u and d. Baryon number is +1/3 per quark. But no free particle with a fractional charge was ever found, and many physicists, Gell-Mann included, treated quarks as a bookkeeping device.

### Step 4: colour (1964–1965)

**The puzzle:** the Δ⁺⁺ is uuu and the Ω⁻ is sss, with all three spins pointing the same way. That puts three identical fermions in the same state, which Pauli's exclusion principle forbids.

Oscar Greenberg (1964), then Moo-Young Han and Yoichiro Nambu (1965), proposed that each quark carries an extra charge with three possible values. It was later called **colour**: red, green and blue. The three s quarks of the Ω⁻ have different colours, so they are not identical, and Pauli is satisfied. (The name is a metaphor: three primary colours combine to make white.)

Colour also explains which combinations exist. Only **colourless** combinations can exist freely: red + green + blue (a baryon) or a colour plus its anticolour (a meson). A lone quark, or two quarks, would carry net colour, and is never seen. Experiments later counted the colours: the decay rate of the neutral pion into two photons, and the rate of hadron production in electron–positron collisions (at ADONE in Frascati and at SLAC), both require exactly three.

### Step 5: seeing inside the proton (1967–1973)

At SLAC in California, a 3 km linear accelerator fired 20 GeV electrons at protons. Jerome Friedman, Henry Kendall and Richard Taylor found far more electrons bouncing back at large angles than a smooth proton allows. It was Rutherford's gold-foil experiment repeated one level deeper. Richard Feynman (1969) interpreted the result: the proton contains point-like pieces, which he called **partons**.

The partons turned out to have spin ½. Neutrino experiments at CERN with the Gargamelle bubble chamber (1973) confirmed their fractional charges and that the proton holds three of them. **The partons were the quarks.** Nobel Prize 1990.

One measurement did not fit: the quarks carry only about half of the proton's momentum. The other half belongs to electrically neutral partons, later identified as **gluons** (chapters 11 and 12).

**The new puzzle:** when struck hard, quarks inside the proton behave as if almost free. Yet no quark ever escapes. How can a force be weak at short distances and unbreakable at long ones? Chapter 11 gives the answer.

## 11. The forces as gauge theories (1954–1973)

Between 1954 and 1973 theorists rewrote all three particle forces in one common language, **gauge theory**, in which a symmetry dictates both the force and its carrier particles. This framework predicted the charm quark, the W and Z bosons, the gluons and the Higgs boson before any of them was seen.

### The idea: symmetry creates forces

In QED the electron's quantum wave has a phase, a kind of internal clock hand. The equations stay the same if you turn that hand by the same amount everywhere: a *global* symmetry. Demand that you may turn it by a different amount at every point in space (a *local* or **gauge** symmetry), and the equations only work if a new field is added to compensate. That field is exactly the electromagnetic field, and its quantum is the photon. **The symmetry requires the force carrier.**

Chen-Ning Yang and Robert Mills (1954) applied the same recipe to richer symmetries, such as isospin. Their theories predict several carriers which, unlike the photon, carry charge themselves and interact with each other. There was a serious flaw: the symmetry demanded that the carriers be massless, but the weak force has a tiny range and needs heavy carriers.

### The Higgs mechanism (1964)

Inspired by Yoichiro Nambu's work on superconductivity, three groups found the way out in 1964: François Englert and Robert Brout; Peter Higgs; and Gerald Guralnik, Carl Hagen and Tom Kibble. Suppose a new field fills all of space and has a non-zero value even in empty space. Then the symmetry still holds in the equations but is hidden in the state of the vacuum (**spontaneous symmetry breaking**). Carriers that interact with this field acquire mass; carriers that do not interact with it stay massless.

Higgs pointed out that the theory leaves a physical trace: a new particle, the ripple of this field itself. It became known as the **Higgs boson**: spin 0, the only fundamental particle of its kind.

### Electroweak unification (1961–1971)

- **Sheldon Glashow (1961)** combined the weak and electromagnetic forces in one symmetry group. It needed four carriers: W⁺, W⁻, a neutral Z⁰ and the photon.
- **Steven Weinberg (1967) and Abdus Salam (1968)** added the Higgs mechanism. The W and Z become heavy, about 85 to 100 times the proton mass, which makes the weak force short-range and "weak". The photon stays massless. The electron and the other matter particles also get their masses from the Higgs field.
- **Gerard 't Hooft and Martinus Veltman (1971)** proved that this theory gives finite, calculable predictions (it is *renormalisable*). From then on physicists took it seriously. Nobel Prizes 1979 and 1999.

The theory made a sharp new prediction: **neutral currents**, weak interactions carried by the Z in which a neutrino scatters and stays a neutrino, without turning into an electron or muon.

### Charm is predicted (1970)

Some kaon decays that change strangeness without changing charge were observed to be extremely rare, far rarer than the three-quark theory predicted. Sheldon Glashow, John Iliopoulos and Luciano Maiani (the **GIM mechanism**, 1970) showed the problem disappears if a fourth quark exists. Called **charm**, it would pair with the strange quark as up pairs with down. Quarks would then form two families, (u, d) and (c, s), matching the two lepton families (chapter 9).

### Neutral currents are found (1973)

In 1973 the Gargamelle bubble chamber at CERN, exposed to a neutrino beam, recorded events in which a neutrino hit an electron or a nucleus and nothing but the neutrino left: no muon. These were the predicted neutral currents. It was the first direct evidence for the Z, and the first major discovery at CERN. Experiments at Fermilab confirmed it after initial disagreement.

### Quantum chromodynamics (1973)

Colour (chapter 10) was made into a gauge symmetry. The resulting theory of the strong force is **quantum chromodynamics (QCD)**, formulated by Harald Fritzsch, Gell-Mann and Heinrich Leutwyler. It has **8 gluons**, and because gluons carry colour themselves, gluons attract other gluons. This one difference from the photon changes everything.

In 1973 David Gross and Frank Wilczek, and independently David Politzer, showed that the strong force becomes **weaker at short distances**. This is **asymptotic freedom**, and it explains why quarks hit hard at SLAC looked almost free. Nobel Prize 2004.

The reverse also holds: at large distances the force does not weaken. Pulling two quarks apart stretches a tube of gluon field like an elastic band. Before the quarks separate, the stored energy creates a new quark–antiquark pair and the tube snaps into two hadrons. This is **confinement**: quarks are never seen alone. In a high-energy collision, a struck quark appears instead as a narrow spray of hadrons called a **jet**.

### The result: the forces of the Standard Model

| Force | Symmetry | Carriers (gauge bosons) | Carrier mass | Range | Acts on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strong | SU(3) colour | 8 gluons | 0 | about 10⁻¹⁵ m (confined) | quarks, gluons |
| Electromagnetic | U(1), what remains after symmetry breaking | photon | 0 | infinite | electrically charged particles |
| Weak | SU(2) × U(1), broken by the Higgs field | W⁺, W⁻, Z⁰ | 80.4 and 91.2 GeV | about 10⁻¹⁸ m | all quarks and leptons |
| Gravity | not part of the Standard Model | graviton (hypothetical) | 0 | infinite | everything with energy |

This is the core of the Standard Model, often written SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1). In 1973 its key ingredients were still predictions: charm, the W and Z, the gluon, the Higgs boson, and (from Kobayashi and Maskawa) a third family. The next forty years were spent finding them.

## 12. Completing the picture (1974–2012)

In 38 years, experiments found every particle the 1973 theory needed, plus a third family that only Kobayashi and Maskawa had anticipated. The last piece, the Higgs boson, was announced on 4 July 2012.

### Charm: the November Revolution (1974)

On 11 November 1974 two teams announced the same new particle, at 3.1 GeV. Samuel Ting's group at Brookhaven (proton beam on a fixed target) called it **J**; Burton Richter's group at SLAC (electron–positron collider SPEAR) called it **ψ**. Within days the ADONE collider in Frascati, pushed beyond its design energy, confirmed it. The name **J/ψ** keeps both.

Its peak was extremely narrow, meaning it lived about a thousand times longer than expected for a particle of that mass. The explanation: it is a **charm quark bound to a charm antiquark** ("charmonium"). Particles containing a single charm quark (D mesons) were found at SLAC in 1976. GIM's prediction was confirmed, and quarks now formed two families like the leptons. Nobel Prize 1976.

### The tau: a surprise third lepton (1975)

At SPEAR, Martin Perl's team saw collisions producing one electron, one muon of opposite charge, and missing energy. The pattern fitted a new heavy lepton, created in pairs, each decaying into an electron or muon plus invisible neutrinos. The **tau (τ)** weighs 1.777 GeV, nearly twice the proton. Nobel Prize 1995.

The tau broke the balance: three lepton families against two quark families. Physicists expected a third quark family, as Kobayashi and Maskawa had already proposed.

### Bottom (1977)

Leon Lederman's team at Fermilab found the **Υ (Upsilon)** at 9.46 GeV, decaying into muon pairs. Like the J/ψ, it is a quark bound to its own antiquark: the **bottom quark**, the first quark of the third family.

### The gluon (1979)

At the PETRA electron–positron collider at DESY in Hamburg, most collisions produced two back-to-back jets: a quark and an antiquark. Some produced **three jets**, in a flat "Mercedes" pattern: the quark or antiquark had radiated a hard gluon, which became the third jet. The TASSO, JADE, MARK-J and PLUTO experiments reported it in 1979. This was the first direct observation of the gluon.

### W and Z (1983)

The W and Z needed collisions at hundreds of GeV. At CERN, Carlo Rubbia proposed turning the SPS accelerator into a proton–antiproton collider. Simon van der Meer's technique of "stochastic cooling" made it possible to collect enough antiprotons. In 1983 the UA1 and UA2 experiments found the **W** (January) and the **Z** (May–June) at the masses electroweak theory predicted. Rubbia and van der Meer received the Nobel Prize in 1984.

### Exactly three families (1989)

The LEP collider at CERN (27 km circumference) and the SLC at SLAC produced millions of Z bosons. The Z can decay into any neutrino type lighter than half its mass, and each type broadens its peak by a fixed amount. The measured width allows **exactly three** light neutrino types. So there are three families of matter, not more (unless a fourth family had an extremely heavy neutrino).

### Top (1995)

The CDF and DØ experiments at Fermilab's Tevatron found the **top quark** at about 173 GeV, as heavy as a gold atom and still the heaviest known fundamental particle. LEP's precision measurements had already indicated its mass range before discovery. The top decays in about 5 × 10⁻²⁵ s, too fast to form hadrons. The third quark family was complete, and when B-factory experiments in Japan and California measured CP violation in B mesons (2001), Kobayashi and Maskawa's explanation was confirmed. They received the Nobel Prize in 2008.

### Tau neutrino (2000)

The DONuT experiment at Fermilab directly observed the tau neutrino through a handful of events in which it produced a tau. All 12 matter particles had now been seen.

### The Higgs boson (2012)

LEP had excluded a Higgs lighter than 114 GeV. The **Large Hadron Collider (LHC)** at CERN, colliding protons in the old LEP tunnel, began physics runs in 2010. On 4 July 2012 the **ATLAS** experiment (spokesperson Fabiola Gianotti) and the **CMS** experiment (spokesperson Joe Incandela) announced a new boson of about 125 GeV, seen decaying into two photons and into four leptons. Later measurements showed spin 0 and couplings proportional to mass, as the Higgs mechanism requires. François Englert and Peter Higgs received the Nobel Prize in 2013.

With the Higgs, every particle of the Standard Model had been observed. The next chapter puts them all in their final places.

## 13. The final classification explained

Every particle can be placed by answering four questions in order. Each question was learned at a different moment of the history: spin in 1925, the strong force in 1947, families between 1962 and 1975, and the role of bosons in 1973.

1. **What is its spin?** A half number makes it a **fermion** (matter); a whole number makes it a **boson** (forces and mass). See chapter 4.
2. **If it is a fermion, does it feel the strong force?** Yes: **quark**. No: **lepton**. See chapters 7 and 8.
3. **Which family (generation)?** Each family is a heavier copy of the first, with identical charges. See chapters 9 and 12.
4. **If it is a boson, is its spin 1 or 0?** Spin 1: a **gauge boson** that carries a force. Spin 0: the **Higgs boson**. See chapter 11.

Composite particles are sorted separately. They are all **hadrons**, and they are named by the quarks they contain.

### Five rules that hold the picture together

1. **Matter is made of fermions; forces are carried by bosons.**
2. **The strong force divides fermions** into quarks and leptons.
3. **Three families, each a heavier copy.** Everyday matter uses only the first family: up and down quarks (in protons and neutrons), electrons, and electron neutrinos from the Sun and radioactivity. Second- and third-family particles decay within a fraction of a second, so they appear only in cosmic rays and accelerators. Nobody knows why there are three, or why their masses differ so much.
4. **Quarks never appear alone**; only colourless hadrons exist freely.
5. **Every particle has an antiparticle** with the same mass and opposite charges.

One more pattern links quarks and leptons. In each family the electric charges add up to zero: three colours × (+2/3 − 1/3) for the quarks, plus −1 for the charged lepton, plus 0 for the neutrino. This cancellation is one of the conditions that keeps the theory mathematically consistent. It suggests that quarks and leptons may share a common origin.

## 14. What the Standard Model does not explain (as of November 2026)

The Standard Model has passed every collider test so far. Yet it does not explain neutrino masses, dark matter, the excess of matter over antimatter, gravity, or the pattern of particle masses. As in the past, these mismatches between theory and observation are where the next particles are most likely to be hiding.

### Neutrinos have mass

- **The puzzle.** From 1968 Ray Davis's Homestake experiment detected only about a third of the neutrinos the Sun should emit.
- **The answer.** Super-Kamiokande in Japan (1998) showed that muon neutrinos made in the atmosphere change type on their way through the Earth. SNO in Canada (2001–2002) showed that the Sun's total neutrino output is correct, but part of it arrives as other types. Changing type (**oscillation**) is possible only if neutrinos have mass. Nobel Prize 2015.
- **Why it matters.** The original Standard Model had massless neutrinos. Their mass may come from the Higgs field, which would need right-handed neutrinos never seen, or the neutrino may be its own antiparticle. That second option is called a **Majorana** particle, after the Italian physicist Ettore Majorana (1937). Experiments search for it through an extremely rare decay, neutrinoless double-beta decay.
- **Status.** In 2025 KATRIN set an upper limit of 0.45 eV on the neutrino mass ([KIT](https://www.kit.edu/kit/english/pi_2025_029_astroparticle-physics-neutrinos-weigh-less-than-0-45-electronvolts.php)), at least a million times lighter than the electron. The JUNO detector in China began taking data in August 2025, and its main goal is to find which neutrino is heaviest, the "mass ordering" ([CAS](https://english.cas.cn/newsroom/headlines/202606/t20260611_1161560.shtml)).

### Dark matter

Galaxies rotate too fast, and galaxy clusters bend light too strongly, for the matter we can see. About 85% of the matter in the universe is invisible ([SLAC](https://www6.slac.stanford.edu/node/6116)). No Standard Model particle fits: neutrinos are too light and too fast. Candidates include WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles) and axions, but none has been detected. The situation resembles the beta-decay crisis of chapter 6: something is missing, and an unseen particle is the simplest explanation.

### Why there is more matter than antimatter

Sakharov's conditions (chapter 9) require CP violation. The Standard Model does have CP violation, from the three quark families, but CERN notes it is many orders of magnitude too small to explain the universe's matter excess ([CERN](https://home.cern/news/news/physics/new-piece-matter-antimatter-puzzle)). New sources are needed; one candidate is CP violation among neutrinos, which future experiments such as DUNE and Hyper-Kamiokande will test.

### Gravity

Gravity is not part of the Standard Model. Between two protons, the electric repulsion is about 10³⁶ times stronger than their gravitational attraction. A quantum of gravity, the **graviton** (a hypothetical boson with spin 2), has never been observed, and no theory of quantum gravity has been tested.

### The pattern of masses

Particle masses range from below 0.45 eV (neutrinos) to 173 GeV (top quark): more than eleven orders of magnitude. Each comes from a separate, arbitrary strength of coupling to the Higgs field. The Standard Model has about 19 free numbers that must be measured rather than predicted. It also does not explain why the Higgs boson is so light compared with the scale where gravity becomes strong (the **hierarchy problem**). Supersymmetry, a popular proposed solution, predicts partner particles; none have been found at the LHC so far.

The muon g-2 measures how much the muon's magnetism exceeds Dirac's value of exactly 2 (chapter 5). Every particle that exists, even unknown ones, nudges this number through quantum effects, which is why it is such a sensitive test.

### What comes next

The High-Luminosity LHC upgrade is the top medium-term priority, and the LHC is expected to run until 2041. On 22 May 2026 the CERN Council named the FCC-ee, a much larger electron–positron collider, as the preferred next flagship project, with a decision targeted for 2028 ([CERN Courier](https://cerncourier.com/cern-council-updates-the-european-strategy/)). It would act as a "Higgs factory", measuring the Higgs boson with high precision.

The history in this document suggests where to look. The neutrino, the muon, charm and the tau each appeared where a measurement disagreed with expectations. Today's disagreements, from dark matter to neutrino masses, are the most likely doors to the next layer.

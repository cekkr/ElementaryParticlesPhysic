The Universe at Different Scales

From Cosmos to Quarks: A Journey of Scale

The universe spans an incredible range of scales. From the observable universe near 10²⁶ meters to the Planck scale near 10⁻³⁵ meters, the span is about 61 orders of magnitude. The Planck length marks a quantum-gravity scale, not an experimentally established minimum distance.

Deeper Understanding

Each step down in scale reveals new physics:

  • 10⁰ m (Human scale): Classical mechanics dominates
  • 10⁻⁹ m (Nanoscale): Quantum effects become noticeable
  • 10⁻¹⁰ m (Atomic): Electron orbitals, chemistry happens here
  • 10⁻¹⁵ m (Nuclear): Strong force dominates, quarks confined
  • 10⁻¹⁸ m (high-resolution probes): Quarks can be treated as weakly coupled in suitable high-momentum-transfer calculations; they still do not emerge as isolated particles.
Mathematical Framework

The de Broglie wavelength sets the quantum scale:

λ = h / p; p ≈ mv only at nonrelativistic speeds

Resolving finer structure requires shorter wavelengths and larger momentum. This motivates high-energy accelerators. Connect wavelength, momentum, and energy.

What is Matter?

The Atomic Theory Evolution

For centuries, we believed atoms were indivisible - the word literally means "uncuttable" in Greek. But the discovery of radioactivity in 1896 changed everything!

Today we know that atoms consist of:

  • Nucleus: Protons and neutrons (made of quarks!)
  • Electron cloud: Electrons in quantum orbitals
  • Scale: The nucleus is much smaller than the atom. The electron cloud is a quantum probability distribution, not little planets on fixed tracks.
Discovery Timeline
  • 1897: J.J. Thomson discovers the electron
  • 1911: Rutherford's gold foil experiment reveals the nucleus
  • 1932: Chadwick discovers the neutron
  • 1964: Gell-Mann and Zweig propose quarks
  • 1974: J/ψ discovery provides evidence for charm
  • 2012: Higgs boson discovered at CERN

CERN Connection

Colliders probe subnuclear structure through high-energy scattering. Heavy-ion collisions can produce quark–gluon plasma; this is different from the static orbital sketch above. Explore quarks and confinement.

The Particle Zoo

Explore the building blocks

The Standard Model: Nature's LEGO Set

An electron in an atom, a photon in a beam of light, and the quarks inside a proton are examples of different elementary particles. Each is an excitation of a quantum field. “Elementary” means no internal structure has been resolved; protons and atoms are composite.

Fermions · matter

Six quarks and six leptons, all spin ½. Identical fermions obey Pauli exclusion. Electrons make shells; up and down quarks build nuclei.

Gauge bosons · interactions

Spin-1 photons, gluons, W and Z bosons mediate electromagnetic, strong, and weak interactions. Identical bosons can share a quantum state.

Higgs · a scalar boson

The spin-0 Higgs is an excitation of the Higgs field. It is a boson but is not an additional gauge force carrier.

The familiar “17 particles” counts grouped entries: W⁺/W⁻ share a tile and eight gluon color states share another. Antiparticles and quark colors are not separately tiled. Gravity is outside this model. CERN: the Standard Model.

Building Blocks: Fermions

From quarks to the matter around you

Up and down quarks carry electric charge and strong-interaction color charge. Color is a quantum label, not a visible color. Observable hadrons are color-neutral combinations. Baryons have three valence quarks; mesons have a valence quark–antiquark pair. Exotic hadrons can have more complex arrangements.

Valence content identifies a hadron; it is not a complete picture of its quarks, antiquarks, and gluons.

Go deeper: confinement and the origin of proton mass

Pulling quarks apart builds a color field; sufficient energy produces new hadrons rather than an isolated quark. At high momentum transfer the strong coupling becomes smaller (asymptotic freedom), allowing perturbative calculations. Confinement dominates at low energies.

A proton has a mass near 938 MeV/c², far larger than the sum of its light valence-quark masses. Most of that mass comes from the dynamics of quarks and gluon fields. The Higgs gives quarks their masses, but does not directly account for most of a proton's mass.

PDG: quantum chromodynamics; CMS: Higgs and ordinary matter.

Leptons and three generations

The electron, muon, and tau have the same electric charge and spin, but very different masses and lifetimes. They do not carry color. Their neutral partners are neutrinos. Generation labels organize families; they are not stages through which a particle ages.

Go deeper: why neutrino “mass” is different

Electron-, muon-, and tau-flavor neutrinos are superpositions of mass states ν₁, ν₂, ν₃. Their relative phases evolve during flight, changing detection probabilities. Oscillations establish nonzero mass-squared differences, not three separately measured flavor masses. They change lepton flavor; they do not by themselves establish violation of total lepton number.

|νₐ⟩ = Σᵢ U*ₐᵢ |νᵢ⟩

The mixing matrix U connects flavor labels to mass labels. The minimal Standard Model has massless neutrinos, so measured oscillations require an extension.

Forces and Their Messengers

Charge tells you which interaction is possible

Electric charge couples to electromagnetism; color charge couples to the strong interaction. Weak interactions involve quarks and leptons, including electrically neutral neutrinos. “Neutral” therefore does not mean “non-interacting.” Select a carrier to connect the force to an actual particle.

Go deeper: exchange diagrams and virtual particles

A Feynman diagram is a contribution to an amplitude, not a literal movie of tiny balls. Internal lines are propagators, often called virtual particles. Four-momentum is conserved at every vertex; virtual lines need not satisfy the free-particle relation E² = p²c² + m²c⁴. The uncertainty principle does not license temporary violations of energy conservation.

Range scale for a massive mediator: ℓ ∼ ℏ / (Mc)

This scale helps explain weak forces. Massless gluons require the separate physics of confinement; a massless carrier alone does not guarantee an observable long-range force.

The Higgs Mechanism

Mass without friction

The Higgs field has a nonzero vacuum value. Its couplings give masses to W/Z bosons and, through Yukawa couplings, charged leptons and quarks. This changes their energy–momentum relation; it does not slow moving particles through drag. The Higgs boson is an excitation of the field, not a cloud of particles that must surround every electron.

Photons remain massless, but carry energy and momentum. The origin of neutrino masses is not settled. Most ordinary matter's mass comes from strong-interaction dynamics inside nuclei, rather than the sum of its elementary constituent masses.

Go deeper: the field value and Yukawa coupling
mᶠ = yᶠ v / √2, with v ≈ 246 GeV (ℏ = c = 1)

A larger Yukawa coupling yᶠ gives a larger fermion mass for the same field value. W/Z masses arise from gauge couplings to the Higgs field. In a common convention, V(Φ) = −μ²Φ†Φ + λ(Φ†Φ)² and ⟨Φ⟩ = (0, v/√2), with v² = μ²/λ. Excitations around this vacuum include the physical spin-0 Higgs.

Experiments infer a Higgs from its decay products and test its couplings, rather than observing the field directly. Explore H → γγ.

Quantum Properties

Spin, identity, and the exclusion principle

Spin is intrinsic angular momentum, not rotation of a tiny solid ball. Identical half-integer-spin particles are fermions; identical integer-spin particles are bosons. Pauli exclusion forbids two identical fermions from occupying the same complete one-particle state. It applies to electrons, quarks, and neutrinos, regardless of their mass or charge.

Two electrons can occupy one spatial orbital if their spin states differ. Many photons can occupy one mode with the same polarization. This contrast helps explain atomic shell structure and the occupation of light modes. Composite particles also have statistics: protons are fermions and pions are bosons.

Go deeper: exchange symmetry and spin measurements
Ψ(1, 2) = −Ψ(2, 1) for identical fermions; + for identical bosons

Putting two identical fermions into exactly the same one-particle state makes the antisymmetric amplitude vanish. This is a statement about quantum states, not a repulsive force or a ban on being at the same position.

S² = ℏ²s(s + 1); S𝓏 = ℏm, with m = −s, …, s

Spin quantum number s and its measured projection m are different. A massive spin-1 particle has three projections; a real massless photon has two transverse helicities, +1 and −1. The photon boxes above represent two independent polarizations, not a third longitudinal mode.

How Particles Interact

Follow a reaction, not just a particle name

A reaction must conserve electric charge, energy, momentum, and angular momentum, and use the available couplings. Decays can proceed through strong, electromagnetic, or weak interactions. Lifetime depends on couplings and phase space, not just mass.

The charge check is necessary, not sufficient. It does not calculate a rate or establish every conservation law.

Go deeper: how diagrams become predictions

Each diagram supplies an amplitude. Add the relevant amplitudes coherently, including their phases, then square the total and integrate over the allowed final-state momenta. A diagram is not an alternative classical path with a separate probability.

Probability ∝ |Σ diagrams ℳ|²; decay width Γ ∝ ∫ dΦ |ℳ|²

The phase-space measure dΦ enforces energy–momentum balance. In natural units τ = 1/Γ; restoring units gives τ = ℏ/Γ. Perturbation theory organizes diagrams in powers of couplings; it becomes unreliable for low-energy strong interactions.

Detecting the Invisible

Read the signature in detector layers

Detectors combine tracks, energy deposits, and decay products to infer particle identities. Charged particles bend in magnetic fields; calorimeters measure deposited energy. Short-lived W, Z, Higgs, and top particles are reconstructed from their products.

Go deeper: reconstruction has ambiguities

A shower is a cascade of secondary particles, not the original particle growing larger. Electron and photon showers look similar; matching a tracker trajectory helps distinguish them. Hadronic showers fluctuate, muons can radiate, and detector acceptance is incomplete. No single layer perfectly identifies every particle.

At a hadron collider the incoming partons' longitudinal momenta are unknown. Missing transverse momentum is therefore especially useful. A missing signal alone is not proof of a neutrino or dark matter.

Go deeper: fields, symmetries, and the Lagrangian

A Lagrangian density specifies fields and their couplings. Schematically the Standard Model has gauge-field terms, fermion motion and gauge couplings, the Higgs kinetic term and potential, and Yukawa interactions. This is an organizational guide, not a fully indexed expression.

ℒSM = ℒgauge + ℒfermion + (DμΦ)†DμΦ − V(Φ) + ℒYukawa

The covariant derivative Dμ packages gauge interactions. The symmetry group SU(3)color × SU(2)left × U(1)hypercharge gives strong and electroweak structure. Electroweak symmetry breaking mixes neutral fields to produce the photon and Z. A photon is not simply an extra, independent unbroken SU(2) carrier.

Locality and symmetries constrain allowed terms; experiments determine parameters such as masses and couplings. A symbolic formula alone does not supply their numerical values.

Beyond the Standard Model

Separate evidence from proposed explanations

The Standard Model is remarkably successful, but is not a complete theory of nature. Neutrino oscillations, dark matter evidence, and the matter–antimatter asymmetry motivate further work; gravity also needs a consistent quantum description.

Established observations do not establish a particular new-particle explanation.
QuestionWhat is establishedWhat is unresolved
NeutrinosFlavor oscillation and nonzero mass differencesAbsolute mass scale and mass-generating mechanism
Dark matterGravitational evidence for additional matterIts microscopic identity; candidates are not discoveries
AntimatterAntiparticles exist; the observable universe favors matterThe mechanism producing the cosmic asymmetry
Go deeper: how a proposed extension is tested

Hypotheses such as axions, supersymmetry, or sterile neutrinos make different predictions. A candidate must fit existing constraints and produce testable consequences; none is confirmed merely because it fills a gap. Collider searches, neutrino experiments, and direct or indirect dark-matter searches probe different assumptions.

Antiparticles have the same mass and spin as their partners and opposite additive charges. The positron is an anti-electron; it is not a negative-mass electron. Whether neutrinos are their own antiparticles remains open.

Quick glossary
Field / particle
A field has degrees of freedom throughout spacetime; a particle is a quantized excitation.
Mass / energy
Rest mass is an invariant property; energy also depends on motion and the reference frame.
Charge
A quantum property governing a coupling; electric charge is only one kind.
State / species
“Electron” names a species. A state also specifies its spatial and spin degrees of freedom.
Flavor / generation
Flavor labels distinguish quarks or leptons; generations group related species.
Gauge / scalar boson
Gauge bosons here have spin 1; the Higgs is a spin-0 scalar.
